The story: a soft fork whose activation doesn't need miners — the enforcement clause is carried by node software. If signaling stays low but lock-in happens anyway, that's the governance boundary made visible: miners can refuse, but they can't stop it. Details: governance-boundary note →
The correction: UASF/URSF are coordination mechanisms — not proof that miners control Bitcoin. A flag-day or a user-resisted fork is a way for node operators to coordinate enforcement, not evidence of who holds power. And miner concentration is a real attack surface — but it doesn't automatically equal protocol control. BIP-110 is the live test of exactly this: the 55% threshold and the mandatory window are coordination parameters, and the outcome tells us who actually moves the valid-state transition boundary.
